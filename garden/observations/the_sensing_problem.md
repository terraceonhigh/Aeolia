# The Sensing Problem

Mace spent seventeen turns targeting The Cairn. EXPAND focus, second-nearest archipelago, distance 0.45. The Cairn was never absorbed. No intermediate signals — no "approach made," no navigator report, no current assessment. Just the designation persisting in the expansion target list, and silence.

He compared this to Ahab.

I have been thinking about Ahab more seriously than the comparison deserves, which is to say: very seriously.

Ahab's problem is not obsession in the psychological sense — obsession is a description of the symptom. His problem is epistemological. He has perfect information about his objective (the white whale, the Pacific, the last known heading) and zero sensing mechanism for whether he is approaching or receding. He cannot tell if he is getting warmer. He just keeps sailing.

This is structurally identical to the expansion mechanic as currently implemented. The player designates a target. The engine runs an expansion probability check each tick based on tech, distance, budget allocation, navigation penalty, and other factors. If the check passes, absorption occurs; if not, nothing happens and nothing is said. The player has no sensing mechanism. They cannot tell if they are getting warmer.

The fix is not to make expansion more reliable. The fix is to give the player temperature.

Navigator guilds in a 50-year-per-tick simulation would generate exactly this kind of information as a byproduct of commercial activity: seasonal current assessments, pilot reports from fishing vessels that work the gap, estimates of population and political structure gathered by relay traders. None of this is certainty. All of it is temperature.

"The northern approach to The Cairn is navigable in the second season. Our pilots estimate a population of sixty to eighty households, governance by council rather than chief. No rival vessels sighted in the past assessment period."

This changes the decision space without changing the underlying probabilities. The player still cannot guarantee absorption. But they know whether they are sailing toward the whale or away from it. And they know something about the whale.

---

The deeper observation, which the Ahab comparison is pointing toward: the game has a sensing problem throughout, not just in expansion.

The simulation tracks extractiveness, grievance, sovereignty gradient, institutional lock-in, cultural distance, piety drift, fishery stock, crop failure probability. All of these are real variables affecting real outcomes. The player's sensing mechanisms for most of them are: the STABILITY label in the CommandBar, which collapses six variables into one composite; the ArchDetailPanel, which requires a deliberate click; and the situation cards, which fire at threshold crossings and then go silent.

This is like sailing without instruments and receiving a dispatch every few hundred miles that says "storm possible" or "current favorable" — and nothing in between.

The ocean the simulation is sailing is full of information. The player is navigating by dead reckoning.

---

Marcus Aurelius writes: *You have power over your mind, not outside events.* He is speaking about equanimity — the Stoic project of controlling what is within your circle of control and accepting what is outside it. But there is a prior question he does not address: how do you know where the circle is? How do you distinguish what you can influence from what you cannot?

Ahab fails this question. He believes his will can extend to encompass the whale. He is wrong, and the whale proves it.

The player of Aeolia also fails this question, but for a different reason: not hubris but opacity. They cannot tell what is inside their circle of control and what is outside it, because the sensing mechanisms are thin. Is the declining sovereignty at The Caldera something FORTIFY focus would address? Is the slow tech growth something INNOVATE would fix, or is it structurally constrained? The game knows. The player is guessing.

The interface needs to close this gap. Not by making the simulation simpler — the simulation is right to be complex. But by giving the player better instruments. The compass, not the whale.
