*Curated for the library at the Architect's request, late April 2026. Sources: W. D. Westervelt, Legends of Ma-ui (Gutenberg #32601); W. D. Westervelt, Legends of Gods and Ghosts (Gutenberg #39195); W. D. Westervelt, Legends of Old Honolulu (Gutenberg #66547); W. D. All public domain.*

## I. The Ocean as Space

Hawaiian, from an ancient chant recorded by Westervelt:

> *Never quiet, never failing, never sleeping,*
> *Never very noisy is the sea of the sacred caves.*

Westervelt, *Legends of Old Honolulu*, on the horizon as cosmological boundary:

> A daring voyager by sailing through the sky-line would break out from this world into an unknown world or a heaven bounded by new horizons.

Westervelt, *Legends of Old Honolulu*, on the surf at Koolau (mele):

> *The surf rises at Koolau,*
> *Blowing the waves into mist,*
> *Into little drops,*
> *Spray falling along the hidden harbor.*

Westervelt, *Legends of Gods and Ghosts*, on the ghost path:

> The ghosts after wandering over the island Oahu would come to this place to find a way into their real home, the Under-world, or, as the Hawaiians usually called it, *Po*.

## II. Islands as Living Things

Westervelt, *Legends of Ma-ui*, the New Zealand version of the island-fishing:

> Out of the great seas the black, ragged head of a large island was rising like a fish.

Westervelt, *Legends of Ma-ui*, on what broke the spell — the brother who looked back:

> The line slacked, snapped, and broke, and the land lay behind them in islands.

Westervelt, *Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes*, on Pele's arrival and settlement:

> She dug day and night and found that this place fulfilled all her desires. Therefore, she fastened herself tight to Hawaii for all time.

Westervelt, *Hawaiian Legends of Volcanoes*, on the primordial conflict of fire and water:

> When the earth seemed ready to open its doors and pour out mighty streams of flowing lava in the defence of Pele, Kama-puaa called for the waters of the ocean to rise up.

## III. Navigation and the Canoe

Westervelt, *Legends of Gods and Ghosts*, the canoe-builder's prayer:

> *Grant a canoe which shall be swift as a fish*
> *To sail in stormy seas*
> *When the storm tosses on all sides.*

Westervelt, *Legends of Ma-ui*, from Queen Liliuokalani's family chant:

> When I let go my hook with divine power, then I get the great Ulua.

Westervelt, *Legends of Gods and Ghosts*, on the channel between islands:

> Between the two islands lay a broad channel about thirty miles wide. When clouds were on the face of the sea, these islands were hidden from each other; but when they lifted, the rugged valleys of the mountains on one island could be clearly seen from the other.

## IV. The Library Stored in Memory

Emerson, *Unwritten Literature of Hawaii*, on hula's cosmological function:

> The hula was a religious service, in which poetry, music, pantomime, and the dance lent themselves, under the forms of dramatic art, to the refreshment of men's minds.

> Its view of life was idyllic, and it gave itself to the celebration of those mythical times when gods and goddesses moved on the earth as men and women and when men and women were as gods.

Emerson, on the kumu-hula and institutional memory:

> The library was stored in one's memory. The language of the mele, which now has become antiquated, then was familiar speech.

> At the king's court...were gathered the bards and those skilled in song, those in whose memories were stored the mythologies, traditions, genealogies, proverbial wisdom, and poetry.

Emerson, on the kumu's method — form as container:

> The kumu's instruction focused on accent, inflection, and all that concerns utterance and vocalization rather than meaning — suggesting form preserved content across generations.

Emerson, on what was lost:

> Within historic times, since the abrogation of the tabu system and the loosening of the old polytheistic ideas, there has been in the hula a lowering of former standards, in some respects a degeneration.

Emerson, from the Laka invocation (Hawaiian and translation):

> *Kupu ka lala, hua ma ka Hikina;*
> *Kupu ka laau ona a Maka-li'i*
>
> (Growth extends, fruit toward the East; grows the tree that bewitches winter fish)

The name-song for the navigator Naihe:

> *Ka nalu nui, a ku ka nalu mai Kona,*
> *Ka malo a ka mahiehie*
>
> (The huge roller surges from Kona, makes loin-cloth fit for a lord)

## V. Ritual Formulas (Hawaiian)

Emerson, from the tabu-lifting ceremony:

> *Noa, noa ia'u* — Freedom, there's freedom to me.

> *Eli-eli kapu! eli-eli noa!* — A tabu profound, a freedom complete!

> *He pule ola* — A prayer for life.

> *E ola, e!* — We hail thee!

## VI. Maori Cosmogony

Shortland, *Maori Religion and Mythology*, on the Maori understanding of creation:

> The *Maori* had no tradition of the Creation. The great mysterious Cause of all things existing in the Cosmos was, as he conceived it, the generative Power.

Shortland, Papa's farewell at the separation of earth and sky (*whakapapa* tradition):

> *Haera ra, e Rangi, ē! ko te wehenga taua i a Rangi.*
> Go, O Rangi, alas! for my separation from Rangi.

Shortland, on Tangaroa, lord of the ocean:

> *Tangaroa* was ancestor of Fish and the *Pounamu*, which is classed with fish by the *Maori*.

Shortland, on whakapapa (genealogy) as institutional technology:

> They often formed the ground-work of their religious formulas, and that to make an error or even hesitation in repeating a *karakia* was deemed fatal to its efficacy.

Shortland, the karakia of Tawaki's ascent:

> *Ascend, O Tawaki, by the narrow path,*
> *By which the path of Rangi was followed;*
> *The path of Tu-kai-te-uru.*

Shortland, Hinenuitepo at the first death:

> Remain, O Tane, to pull up our offspring to Day; while I go below to drag down our offspring to Night.
