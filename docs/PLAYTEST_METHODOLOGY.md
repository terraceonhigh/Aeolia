# Playtest Methodology: Blind Reading → Reveal → Play

*Established April 2026. Standard protocol for Aeolia playtesting.*

---

## Why This Protocol Exists

Conventional playtesting asks: "Is this game fun? Is it clear? Does it work?" These are useful questions for a conventional game. Aeolia is not a conventional game. It is a simulation engine grounded in institutional economics, with a worldbuilding corpus that makes the same arguments the mechanics make — through different media and in different registers.

The standard Aeolia playtest protocol asks a different set of questions: Does the game deliver the world the texts built? Does playing teach what reading teaches? Where does the procedural rhetoric (the argument-through-rules) match the verbal rhetoric (the argument-through-prose), and where do they diverge?

To answer these questions, the playtester must encounter the world *before* encountering the game.

---

## The Protocol

### Phase 1: Blind Reading

The playtester reads a corpus track without knowing the project is a game. They are told only: "Someone has handed you a collection of documents from a project called Aeolia. You don't know what the project is. Just read and react."

**Three tracks (assign one per playtester):**

**Track A — Garden Corpus:**
- `garden/GARDEN_INDEX.md` (orientation)
- 8-10 garden observations (start with `the_strange_equilibrium.md`, `seven_structural_ironies.md`, `the_thalassocratic_condition.md`)
- 6-8 garden fragments (must include `maaruf_house_private_log_y31.md`, `dispatch_year_zero.md`, `vaanthi_petition_y96.md`, `lyceum_discussion_notes_y42.md`)
- 3-5 parables (must include `the_relay_node.md`, `the_second_charter.md`)

**Track B — Reach Textbooks:**
- `aeolia-godot/optimization/worldbuilding/ch1.md` through `ch12.md` (Intro to Reach History)
- `aeolia-godot/optimization/worldbuilding/guide_ch3-6.md`, `guide_ch7.md`, `guide_ch8.md` (Our Reach, Our Trade)

**Track C — Lattice Textbooks:**
- `aeolia-godot/optimization/worldbuilding/lattice_ch1.md` through `lattice_ch10.md` (Intro to Lattice History)
- `aeolia-godot/optimization/worldbuilding/circuit_ch1.md` through `circuit_ch9.md` (The Circuit and the Contract)
- 3-4 institutional fragments (`sovereignty_commerce_council_y98.md`, `laamu_basin_regrets_y100.md`, `maaruf_house_private_log_y31.md`)

**Phase 1 prompts (after reading, before reveal):**
1. What is this? What do you think the project is, based only on what you've read?
2. What stayed with you? What's the emotional register? What's the intellectual project?
3. What surprised you?
4. If this project could become any artifact — book, course, museum, film, anything — what form do you think it wants to be?

### Phase 2: Reveal

Tell the playtester: "It's a video game. A turn-based civilization strategy game in a web browser. You're going to play it now."

**Phase 2 prompts (immediate reaction, before playing):**
1. How do you feel about that?
2. What do you expect to find?
3. What would disappoint you?

### Phase 3: Play

The playtester plays 15-20 turns of the strategy game. They should:
- Set speed to pause initially so they can read everything
- Read ALL dispatches, situation cards, and event popups carefully
- Make decisions — pick a National Focus, respond to cards, set expansion targets
- Take notes on what they recognize from the texts and what they don't

**Phase 3 prompts (after playing):**
1. What was the experience of encountering this world as a game, having read the texts first?
2. What moments connected to what you read? Where did you think "that's the world I know"?
3. Where were you disappointed? Where did the game fail to deliver what the texts promised?
4. What does the game do that the texts couldn't? Is there anything you understood better for having played?
5. One specific change to better honor the world the texts built.

## Why Blind Reading First

The April 2026 sessions tested two approaches:

**Approach A (informed):** Readers told upfront the project is a game. Asked to read the texts and then evaluate the game against them.
- Result: Clipboard mode. Readers catalogued what was present and absent. Useful diagnostics but the emotional posture was assessment, not encounter.

**Approach B (blind):** Readers given the texts with no context. Formed their own impression of what the project was. Then surprised with the game.
- Result: Encounter mode. Readers built a relationship with the world first, then the game arrived as something to feel rather than evaluate. Produced fundamentally different (and more productive) insights.

Key findings unique to Approach B:
- "The game is the texts' negative space" — the texts deliver the tragedy, the game delivers the constraint
- "The punchline structure" — the game makes "winning means standing still" land as the conclusion of a 10,000-year argument rather than an aphorism
- "The resolution limit is honest, not a failure" — at 50-year resolution, the person in the Maaruf log doesn't exist, only the pattern exists
- Players who read the texts first interpreted early-game constraint as the game's argument (bounded agency) rather than as a design failure

Approach B produces *structural criticism* — feedback about whether the game's procedures make the same arguments as the texts. Approach A produces *feature-list evaluation* — feedback about what's present and absent.

Both are useful. Approach B is standard for Aeolia because the project's goal is structural: the game and the texts should be making the same argument through different media.

## Theoretical Grounding

The protocol operationalizes Bogost's *simulation gap* (2007): the space between rule-based representation and player subjectivity is where meaning is made. Pre-loading the player's subjectivity with the textbooks doesn't corrupt the test — it calibrates the gap. The player who has read the Reach civics series and then encounters the colonial resistance card's "everyday resistance" framing is experiencing the simulation gap with maximum information. Their feedback tells you whether the procedures match the claims.

Sicart's critique ("Against Procedurality," 2011) is also relevant: meaning doesn't live purely in procedures but in the player's ethical engagement with them. Blind readers bring ethical engagement from the texts into the game, which changes what the game means.

## Practical Notes

- **Agent-based playtesting works.** The April 2026 sessions used Sonnet agents as readers/players. Agents can read the full corpus, play through browser automation, and write substantive assessments. Three agents per phase is the standard panel size.
- **One track per agent.** Don't mix tracks — each reader should have a single coherent perspective (Garden analyst, Reach historian, Lattice citizen).
- **The reveal is the data.** The Phase 2 reaction — "how do you feel about discovering this is a game?" — is often the most diagnostic moment. Guard it carefully. Don't hint at the game during Phase 1.
- **Play time matters.** 15-20 turns minimum. Shorter sessions don't reach first expansion or first contact. The game's arguments take time to develop.
- **The marginalia are not part of the protocol.** They are Clio's private working notes and should not be shown to playtesters.
